WOMEN FOUNDER GRANTS FUNDING

Women founders made up just 8.6% of recent Industry Growth Program grants

Startup Daily · 3d ago

A new analysis of Australia's Industry Growth Program found that women-led companies received just 8.6% of grants — despite making up a larger share of applicants. The program has no explicit gender targets, and the gap suggests structural bias may be operating at the selection stage rather than the application stage. For any founder seeking non-dilutive capital, the data is a direct reminder that government funding isn't neutrally distributed. The open question is whether the gap reflects panel composition, pitch framing norms, or sector concentration in industries where women are already underrepresented.

Claude's Take
The 8.6% figure is a policy failure masquerading as a pipeline problem. The framing that women aren't applying enough tends to obscure the fact that the selection process itself has embedded preferences — and this data makes that harder to ignore.

Plate I  ·  Oil on canvas
Interior of St. Mark's, Venice
David Dalhoff Neal  ·  1869

Neal painted this vast interior during an era when Venice was the obligatory pilgrimage for ambitious American artists. What's surprising is where his eye goes: not up to the famous Byzantine mosaics, but across the nave, where human figures diminish against gilded arches until they become scale markers rather than subjects. There's something quietly American about the choice — this cataloguing impulse, this need to possess through paint what cannot be carried home. The building is what he came to describe. The people are just evidence of how large it is.

Plate II  ·  Etching with drypoint on ivory laid paper
The Fall of the Giants
Salvator Rosa  ·  1663

Rosa added his own sardonic Latin inscription to this etching: "They are raised up high that they may be hurled down" — written during years of professional frustration in Rome. Jupiter's thunderbolt here reads less like divine justice than cosmic indifference; the giants fall not because they were wicked but because they reached too high. What lingers is the suspicion that Rosa identified with the falling figures rather than the god. The etching has the quality of a self-portrait in which the artist is too honest to cast himself as the hero.

Substack Brief

The newsletters this week are circling one question: what do you build when the old system's expiration date is finally visible?

Lenny's Newsletter (Elena Verna)
You'll lose your job in 2027.
Elena Verna's argument is blunt: assume your current role is close to its expiration date, and act accordingly now rather than waiting to be forced. The piece reframes instability as strategic opportunity for those willing to build career optionality before the disruption hits. Her three-part prescription: assess whether you're genuinely excited about your work, build solopreneurship momentum through consulting or SaaS (she discloses earning more from her solo practice than her full-time salary), and develop AI-native skills beyond the writing-assistant level. The cost of software development is disappearing, which means the technical barriers that once separated founders from non-technical people are dissolving.
The founders who will win the next cycle aren't the ones who survive the layoffs — they're the ones who treated their current job as an optionality-building exercise.
Content angle: "Your Job Is Your Runway, Not Your Destination" — a Substack piece arguing that the Verna framework applies even to people already building: treat consulting income as a signal the market values your judgment.

Inga Grinko's Substack
The Great Unboxing: Why AI Might Be the Biggest Opportunity of Our Lifetime
Between 118,000 and 220,000 jobs have been cut globally since January 2025, from Microsoft to LVMH to Burberry — and Grinko's central move is to flip the frame: this isn't the end of opportunity, it's the end of a system that trained people to be "good system participants" rather than creators. The system — diploma, title, company name, climbing one ladder for thirty years — was never security; it was the illusion of security, and AI is simply making the illusion visible faster. What's emerging in its place is the first era in history where a single creative person can access the production infrastructure that previously required a company. The essay is a permission slip written at systemic scale.
AI is not just automating jobs — it's dismantling the monopoly that large organizations held on the tools needed to do meaningful work at scale.
Content angle: "The Permission Slip Nobody Gave You: What 220,000 Layoffs Are Actually Saying" — a response essay arguing the solo founder path isn't a consolation prize, it's the structural upgrade.

First to Market (Alex Garberson)
Being Louder Won't Build Your Brand. Make Your Ideas Travel Like This.
Three practitioners — Dianne Wilson, Alicia Teltz, and Shreya Vaidya — converge on one insight: a founder brand isn't built by being louder, it's built by distilling a unique worldview into ideas portable enough for others to borrow and carry. The essay offers a six-test pressure check for any brand idea: Is it unique enough to repel some people? Borrowable enough that someone would use your language in a meeting? Repeatable across formats without making you miserable? The common misstep across all three contributors is outsourcing thinking to AI before you've established your actual perspective — using tools to amplify before you've defined what you're amplifying.
A brilliant point of view that stays in your head isn't a brand — it becomes one only when someone else starts using your language to describe their own experience.

Female Founders Only
Female Founder Friday: Amanda Jane Jones
Designer, illustrator, and author Amanda Jane Jones — co-author of Mother/Founder — challenges the premise that women must choose between career ambition and being present at home, arguing that the either/or framing is a cultural construction rather than a practical reality. Jones and her husband both pursued meaningful work and parenting simultaneously, concluding that balance isn't a formula but an ongoing practice of intentionality about what matters most in a given season. The interview surfaces a nuanced argument about partnership: the model that works isn't one partner sacrificing career for family presence, but two people who both value work and family and build structures that honor both.
Balance isn't a destination you reach — it's a practice of staying intentional about what matters most in each season of building.

Plate III  ·  Oil on canvas
Self-Portrait
Walter Shirlaw  ·  1878

Shirlaw painted this at forty, fresh from eight years of study in Munich — the necessary pilgrimage for American painters who couldn't afford Paris. The brushwork reads as argument rather than record: look how European I've become. He was one of the founding members of the Society of American Artists, formed that same year in direct opposition to the conservative National Academy, and this portrait feels like a calling card for that insurgency. The face of a man who has decided, at last, what kind of artist he intends to be.

Plate IV  ·  Etching in reddish brown on paper
Rest on the Flight into Egypt
Bartolomeo Biscaino  ·  n.d.

Biscaino died before he was thirty, and nearly all his work carries this compressed, feverish Mannerist quality — figures stretched upward as though gravity had loosened its grip. The angel gathering fruit here is doing something domestic and ordinary in the middle of a sacred escape, which is the Mannerist sleight of hand: transcendence rendered mundane, the miraculous folded into the texture of a rest stop. The print's reddish-brown tone — unusual for an etching — gives the scene an unexpected warmth that works against its formal elongation, as if Biscaino couldn't quite commit to the coolness the style demanded.

SMALL BUSINESS POLICY
New SBA policy would bar green-card holders from small business loans
The Business Journals · Yesterday
The Small Business Administration is advancing a proposed rule that would make permanent residents — green-card holders — ineligible for SBA-backed loans, including the flagship 7(a) and 504 programs. This reverses decades of policy that treated lawful permanent residents similarly to citizens for lending purposes. The affected population is substantial: immigrant founders represent a disproportionately high share of high-growth startups and small businesses in the US, and many relied on SBA loans during early stages. If finalized, the rule would effectively close one of the few accessible capital channels for founders who haven't yet naturalized, pushing them toward more expensive private credit or away from the SBA ecosystem entirely.

Black-led founding teams raised their highest quarterly capital total since Q2 2022, a milestone that looks encouraging on the surface but comes with a significant asterisk. WOMEN FOUNDER GRANTS FUNDING
Women founders made up just 8.6% of recent Industry Growth Program grants
Startup Daily · 14d ago
A new analysis of the Industry Growth Program's recent grant cycle found that women-led businesses received just 8.6% of total awards, a figure dramatically lower than their share of applicants and of the overall small business population. The gap persists despite explicit commitments from the program to improve gender equity outcomes in its disbursements. Researchers point to selection criteria that inadvertently favor capital-intensive sectors where men are historically overrepresented, as well as review panel composition that skews male. The finding adds to a growing body of evidence that non-dilutive government grants — often positioned as the more equitable alternative to venture capital — replicate many of the same structural biases.

AI POLICY REGULATION
The U.S. has 1,200 AI bills and no good test for any of them
Fortune · 20d ago
Fortune's investigation finds that approximately 1,200 AI-related bills have been introduced across federal and state legislatures in the US, with no standardized framework for evaluating their impact or effectiveness. The legislative explosion spans everything from AI liability in healthcare to content moderation requirements for AI-generated media, creating a patchwork that makes compliance nearly impossible to plan for at the startup stage. Experts note that the absence of a coherent evaluation standard means legislators are essentially guessing, often duplicating or conflicting with one another across jurisdictions. For AI startups selling into regulated industries — healthcare, finance, education — this uncertainty directly delays enterprise sales cycles and raises legal costs before a single deal closes.

SG Decypher
Stop Blaming the Fire
The piece makes a structurally uncomfortable argument: blanket opposition to textile incineration is symbolic politics, not environmentalism, and it distracts from the actual barriers blocking circular fashion progress. The volume of textile waste being incinerated vastly exceeds any realistic near-term recycling throughput, which means incineration is currently functioning as a relief valve on an overloaded system — not a primary disposal strategy — and shutting it down doesn't automatically redirect investment into recycling infrastructure. Modern Scandinavian waste-to-energy facilities operate with sophisticated emission controls and coexist with thriving recycling innovation in the same cities, directly contradicting the either/or frame. The real constraints on textile recycling scaling are unglamorous: blended fabric compositions that resist mechanical processing, underdeveloped sortation infrastructure, chemical contamination from dyes and elastane, and the absence of reliable offtake contracts from brands. The conclusion is that productive progress requires treating incineration as a managed transition tool while simultaneously building the design, collection, and processing systems that could eventually replace it.
Demonizing incineration lets the industry perform environmental commitment without addressing the feedstock quality, sortation, and offtake contract problems that actually determine whether recycling scales.

All Things Fashion Tech
Can a Sixty-Year-Old Weave Fix One of Fashion's Next Gen Materials?
The piece argues that the repeated commercial failure of mushroom leather isn't primarily a biotechnology problem — it's a structural durability problem that may be solvable by applying proven craft methods rather than waiting for biological improvements. Bottega Veneta's decision to apply their 60-year-old intrecciato weaving technique to mycelium leather represents an under-discussed design hypothesis: that traditional construction methods can compensate for the physical limitations of new materials by redistributing stress across a woven structure rather than relying on the material's tensile strength alone. The implication is that sustainable material development has been too narrowly focused on the lab and not enough on the hands-on manufacturing knowledge that has kept leather goods structurally sound for centuries. The conclusion is suggestive rather than definitive — the article is partly paywalled — but the frame it offers is genuinely useful: craft and biotech as complementary rather than sequential problem-solving layers.
The durability gap in sustainable materials may be a craft problem, not a chemistry problem — and fashion houses with decades of construction technique are sitting on under-deployed IP.

Circular Fashion News
Circular Fashion News / June 1: Funding for upcycling, Zalando x Vestiaire, Textile sorting solution wins eBay Fund
The newsletter argues through accumulation that circular fashion has moved from innovation theater into commercial infrastructure, and the evidence comes from multiple simultaneous signals rather than a single headline. Losanje raised €6.7 million for upcycling capacity; Solena Materials secured €1.4 million for protein fiber development; Trosort won the eBay Circular Fashion Fund for textile sorting technology — all in the same two-week window. The Zalando x Vestiaire Collective partnership is the most structurally significant: it reframes resale platforms as supply infrastructure for major retailers rather than competitors, a shift that changes the business model logic of secondhand at scale. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's coordinated policy push — 69 organizations calling for reduced VAT on resale and repair, lower labor taxes, and Extended Producer Responsibility — is the most consequential long-term development, with projections showing these changes could raise gross profit margins to 55% for resale and 41% for repair. Mud Jeans discontinuing its leasing model in favor of resale and repair is a quiet but important data point: the first-generation circular models are being replaced by ones that are actually economically viable.
Zalando treating Vestiaire as a supply partner — not a competitor — is the clearest sign yet that resale is moving from alternative channel to core retail infrastructure.

Venture Capital Archive
VCA#055: The Future is Already Funded
The essay's central claim is that VC has structurally front-run adoption: capital has chosen its winners in AI infrastructure and defence tech, but market readiness is lagging years behind the investment thesis. The defence-tech section is the most rigorously argued — Europe funded $8.7 billion in defence startups in 2025, up 55% year-over-year, but 27 separate national procurement systems, a 16-year track record in Germany of failing to integrate drones on corvettes, and 4-5 year minimum contracting timelines mean the capital arrived before the institutional capacity to deploy it existed. The author introduces a useful distinction between 'macro thesis disguised as sector thesis' — bets on defence-tech that are really bets on procurement reform, not technology — and genuine product bets. On AI, the concentration argument is clean: foundation models now anchor top-performing portfolios, returns concentrate around compute, infrastructure, and developer tooling, and power-law dynamics make luck versus skill in venture selection essentially indistinguishable. The piece closes on women moving from founder pipelines to investor roles as 'representation to ownership,' flagging it as a structural shift rather than a trend story.
Capital concentration in AI and defence-tech isn't a bubble signal — it's a timing mismatch signal: money moved fast, institutions move slow, and the returns depend on the gap closing.

Melissa Kwan
This month's good stuff
Melissa Kwan makes a deceptively simple argument: you cannot produce your best work from scarcity and frustration, and the output quality difference between working from obligation and working from desire is visible even when the external conditions — product, market, team — remain unchanged. The piece documents her year of burnout recovery, noting that the vicious cycle wasn't caused by failure but by the loss of desire itself: disinterest prevented completion, incomplete work deepened dissatisfaction, and dissatisfaction killed motivation further. The turning point wasn't an external trigger but a reconnection with the intrinsic pleasure of building, which she frames through a distinction between fulfilling obligations versus pursuing purpose. The practical implication for founders is uncomfortable: if you've stopped wanting to do the work, changing your strategy, your market, or your metrics won't fix it. The philosophy she arrives at — 'when you do things from love and abundance, something different shows up entirely' — is less self-help than diagnosis: the quality of your desire is upstream of everything else.
Burnout isn't about volume of work — it's about working from scarcity, and the only fix is reconnecting with what you genuinely want to build, not optimizing harder.

Code Like a Girl
When AI Does the Execution, What's Left for You?
The essay documents a structural shift in data science work that has already happened: AI has moved the bottleneck from implementation capacity to decision-making quality, and the author frames this not as a future risk but as a present reality she's living inside. Time that used to go to SQL writing and dashboard building now goes to stakeholder conversations, assumption audits, and framing problems tightly enough for AI to produce grounded outputs. The piece identifies what remains irreducibly human: direct conversations with stakeholders before any implementation begins, manual data validation across sources, experiment design, and the domain judgment to distinguish meaningful metrics from easy-to-measure noise. The cross-functional UX research project she describes is the clearest evidence — rapid prototyping became feasible because execution was no longer the limiting factor, which accelerated the work but also raised the stakes on the quality of the framing. The conclusion is not reassuring in the conventional sense: execution-focused roles are becoming insufficient, and future value in data science comes from being an orchestrator of AI systems rather than an implementation specialist.
The data science work AI can't replace isn't the technical work — it's the judgment work: knowing which problem is worth solving and what 'good enough' looks like given the business context.

Karo Zieminski
Claude Opus 4.8: What Changed, and How I'll Test It
The review frames Opus 4.8 as a deliberate bridge rather than a flagship — 'not the main event, but the model that teaches you how to work before Mythos arrives' — and most of its value comes from that framing rather than raw capability claims. The key improvements are honest-calibration and inspectability: the model is approximately four times less likely than Opus 4.7 to miss flaws in its own code, and Dynamic Workflows enable parallel subagent orchestration with verifiable handoffs. The author is rigorously skeptical about benchmarks, noting 'benchmarks are numbers produced by a testing method' before presenting SWE-Bench and GDPval-AA improvements alongside methodological caveats. The practical decision framework she provides is more useful than the benchmark section: use Dynamic Workflows for large migrations and multi-step verifiable tasks; avoid them for vague requests like 'make it better' because orchestration overhead burns tokens without structured verification loops. Fast Mode at one-third the previous cost changes the economics of high-volume iterative work. The essay's honest limitation is that it reviews a model's potential testing framework rather than completed tests — she outlines what she intends to measure for honesty, code self-doubt, and workflow reviewability, but hasn't yet run those evaluations.
Opus 4.8's real value isn't the benchmark jump — it's that the model rewards teams who design inspectable, verifiable workflows rather than those asking large models vague questions.
